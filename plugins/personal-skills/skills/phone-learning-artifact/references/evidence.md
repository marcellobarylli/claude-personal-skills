# The instructional-design evidence, verified

Scope filter: **comprehension, transfer and cognitive load on a single pass**. Findings whose
only established outcome is delayed free recall are out of scope — they belong to the
memorisation literature, which is a different task with different rules.

Every claim below was checked against primary sources and meta-analyses. The first draft of
this list was mostly wrong, which is the reason the file exists: the intuitive,
widely-repeated design principles are largely the ones that fail. Where an independent
meta-analysis disagrees with the originating lab's own figure, **quote the independent one**.

## 1. Use these six

### 1. No reading unit requires scrolling or panning

**Clinton-Lisell, V., & Litzinger, C. (2026).** Decoding digital reading: a network
meta-analysis of comprehension across devices. *Education and Information Technologies,
31*(6), 1611–1643. `10.1007/s10639-025-13843-8` — 56 samples, 79 effects, five conditions.

> **Scrolling necessary: paper better by *g* = 0.35–0.48. No scrolling: no reliable
> difference, *g* = 0.03–0.12.**

This relocates the whole paper-versus-screen literature onto a single actionable variable.
Context: Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmerón (2018), *Educational Research Review, 25*,
23–38, `10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003` — 54 studies, 171,055 participants, *g* = −0.21
[−0.28, −0.14]; moderators were time frame (limited −0.26 vs self-paced −0.09), genre
(informational −0.27, **narrative +0.01**) and publication year (growing 0.01/yr).

⚠️ Device type was **not** a significant moderator. Smartphones rank last in the 2026 network
meta-analysis but on **only three studies**, with both scrolling subgroups non-significant
and the small-screen explanation the authors' own conjecture. Salmerón et al. (2024),
`10.1037/edu0000830`, found handhelds at about half the computer effect — and **explicitly
excluded mobile phones**. So: claim the scrolling result, not a phone-specific number.

### 2. Cut extraneous material

**Sundararajan, N., & Adesope, O. (2020).** *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(3), 707–734.
`10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4` — seductive details *g* ≈ −0.33 to −0.41.

The best-evidenced lever that is also *subtractive*, which makes it the cheapest. Note Mayer's
own coherence figure (*d* = 0.86) is a median over his lab; use the independent number.

### 3. Signal structure

**Alpizar, D., Adesope, O., & Wong, R. (2020).** *ETR&D, 68*(5), 2095–2119.
`10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7` — *d* = 0.38.

Modest but real. Headings, position indicators, explicit structure cues.

### 4. Diagrams *alongside* prose, not instead of it

**Cromley, J. G., & Chen, R. (2025).** A meta-analysis of Richard Mayer's multimedia learning
research. *Educational Research Review, 49*, 100730. `10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730` — 92
articles, 181 studies, 591 effects; overall *g* = 0.37, declining per year, eight principles
non-significant. **Text+Diagrams *g* = 0.45 [0.32, 0.57]** — the largest, most consistent
media type.

The multimedia principle tests **adding** a graphic to text, never replacing text with one.
An instinct to "turn the prose into a diagram" inverts the finding.

### 5. A compact table can beat informationally matched prose

**Brick, C., McDowell, M., & Freeman, A. L. J. (2020).** Risk communication in tables versus
text: a registered report randomized trial on "fact boxes". *Royal Society Open Science,
7*(3), 190876. `10.1098/rsos.190876` — pre-registered, *N* = 2,305 census-matched UK adults,
**informationally matched prose control**: comprehension 79.6% vs 69.7%, *d* = 0.39
[0.31, 0.47], consistent across numeracy and education.

⚠️ The paper does not identify which items required cross-option comparison. Claim *"a compact
table improved comprehension over matched prose"* — **not** *"tables beat prose for relational
reasoning."*

### 6. Remove time pressure; never frame it as preliminary

**Ackerman, R., & Goldsmith, M. (2011).** *JEP: Applied, 17*(1), 18–32. `10.1037/a0022086` —
**time imposed: no media difference. Time free: screen 63.2% vs paper 72.3%, *d* = 0.79**,
with overconfidence +10.4 vs +2.3 and erratic stopping *d* = 0.95.

Supported by Delgado et al.'s largest significant moderator, and by **Sidi, Shpigelman,
Zalmanov & Ackerman (2017)**, *Learning and Instruction, 51*, 61–73 — screen inferiority
appeared when the task was framed as *preliminary / less important*, even under a loose time
frame. Lauterman & Ackerman (2014), `10.1016/j.chb.2014.02.046` — in-depth processing
eliminated it.

**This is the largest available lever and it is not a layout decision.** It is why the
artifact carries no time estimate, no completion bar, and presents itself as the real reading.

⚠️ One incidental finding worth respecting: Ackerman & Goldsmith's sub-group required to
self-assess **every 3 minutes** added so much variance it was excluded from the study-time
analyses. That is the single datum on mid-reading prompts, and it is a cost.

## 2. Refuse these nine

### 1. Segmenting into learner-paced chunks

Rey et al. (2019), *EPR, 31*(2), 389–419, `10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4`: retention *d* = 0.32,
transfer *d* = 0.36 — but **learner-paced retention *d* = 0.19, *p* = .10 (n.s.)** and
**learner-paced cognitive load *d* = 0.08, *p* = .43 (n.s.)**; allowing reordering extinguished
it. The corpus is **narrated animation** (average segment length 75.49 s; mixed audio-visual in
54 of 88 comparisons; the authors concede they never coded material type). *g* = 0.19 n.s. in
Mayer's own corpus.

**The mechanism — interrupting a stream that outruns the reader — does not exist in static
text, which is already learner-paced.**

### 2. Pre-training

**No meta-analysis exists.** *g* = 0.28 n.s. in Mayer's own corpus. No experiment testing it
on static technical text was found. An evidence gap, not a principle.

### 3. Split attention as a reason to avoid prose cross-references

Schroeder, N. L., & **Cenkci**, A. T. (2018), *EPR, 30*(3), 679–701,
`10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9`: overall *g* = 0.63, transfer *g* = 0.65, *k* = 37. But the
inclusion criterion, verbatim: *"We did not consider moving relevant text closer to other
on-screen text to be an integrated design when there were no relevant images involved."*
**Zero text-only comparisons.** Their redundancy moderator is significant (*Q*b = 11.18,
*p* = .01): not-redundant *g* = 0.72; **diagram-intelligible-alone *g* = 0.26 [−0.34, 0.85],
n.s.** — a self-contained table is that cell. Schroeder & Cenkci (2020), `10.1037/edu0000372`,
reviewed 41 comparisons and found the load measures *largely do not support* the extraneous-load
account.

⚠️ The circulating *d* = 0.85 pools spatial **and** temporal contiguity; never cite it as
spatial. Avoiding "see §4" is fine on usability grounds — just don't claim evidence for it.

### 4. Pretesting / prediction prompts

**St. Hilaire, K. J., Chan, J. C. K., & Ahn, D. (2024).** *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 31*,
411–441. `10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8` — specific *g* = **0.54** [0.42, 0.66], *k* = 97;
general *g* = **0.04** [−0.04, 0.11], *k* = 91, *p* = .349. **By material: audiovisual 0.17 vs
TEXT −0.005, *p* = .017.** Replicated by King-Shepard et al. (2025),
`10.1007/s10648-025-10075-7`: repeated *g* = 0.66, **new *g* = 0.01**.

Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), `10.1037/a0016496`, verbatim: *"The benefit of testing did not
spread to untested items, but neither did it hurt."* Brod (2021), `10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1`,
is a theoretical review **explicitly limited to simple declarative learning**.

### 5. Progressive disclosure / tap-to-reveal

**McDonald, A. M., Reeder, R. W., Kelley, P. G., & Cranor, L. F. (2009).** *PETS 2009*, LNCS
37–55. `10.1007/978-3-642-03168-7_3` — ***N* = 749**. Verbatim: *"participants were faster with
standardized formats but at the expense of accuracy for layered policies"*; *"the layered
notice effectively hides information and reduces transparency."* And: *"Participants found
layered easier to understand even though they were less accurate."*

Krenca, Taylor & Deacon (2024), `10.1111/1467-9817.12468`: **hover-for-definition hyperlinks
lowered comprehension**; scrolling did not.

🎯 The one positive signal, and the basis for the overlay pattern: DeStefano & LeFevre (2007),
`10.1016/j.chb.2005.08.012`, report that **labelled** previews helped, because an unlabeled
link *"tells merely that more information is available, but not how the linked text is
related."* So an expander's **closed state must already carry the proposition and its relation
to the argument**, such that a reader who never expands still gets a coherent text.

⚠️ DeStefano & LeFevre is a **narrative review of 38 studies, not a meta-analysis**, and its
"more links → worse learning" claim is overstated: Madrid et al. (2009),
`10.1016/j.chb.2008.06.005`, tested 3 vs 8 links and found no effect.

### 6. Optimal line length (45–75 characters)

Craft convention, and the record is worse than "weakly evidenced":

| Figure | Actual source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 66 / 45–75 | Bringhurst, *Elements of Typographic Style* | craft, asserted not derived |
| ≤70 cpl | Spencer, *The Visible Word* (1968) | craft book |
| 52 cpl | Rayner & Pollatsek (1989) textbook | re-reading of Tinker; nobody ran a study producing 52 |
| ~57 cpl | Tinker & Paterson (1929) | measured **speed in print** |
| ≤80 chars | WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.8 | "Studies have shown", **no citation** |

Dyson (2004), `10.1080/01449290410001715714`, the standard synthesis, verbatim: *"Few studies
specifically address the effect of line length on comprehension"* and *"Most of the studies on
line length report faster reading with longer lines."* Duchnicky & Kolers (1983): no
comprehension difference. Dyson & Kipping (1998): 100 cpl faster than 25, no comprehension
difference, 55 rated easiest. Shaikh & Chaparro (2005): 95 cpl fastest, no main effect on
comprehension. Atilgan, Xiong & Legge (2020), `10.1073/pnas.2007514117`: **~13 characters per
line already buys 80% of maximum reading speed** (speed, not comprehension).

**Say line length has never been shown to matter — not that it has been shown not to.**

🎯 The robust generalisation here is about users, not layout: **preference does not predict
performance**, across six independent studies. Muter & Maurutto (1991) bundled nine
recommended settings against plain text: no difference in rate or comprehension, but 13 of 18
preferred the enhanced format.

### 7. Pagination over scrolling

**Joshi, N., Casiez, G., & Vogel, D. (2025).** Is pagination better than scrolling when reading
on a phone? *CHI EA '25*. `10.1145/3706599.3720178` — *N* = 100 on their own phones:
comprehension 14.8 vs 14.7/20, **n.s.**; duration and mental demand also n.s.

Sanchez & Wiley (2009), `10.1177/0018720809352788`, is the usual citation, but ⚠️ the effect
rests on Experiment 1 alone (*N* = 40); **Experiment 2 did not replicate the main effect**, and
its paginated condition **confounds pagination with segmentation at meaning boundaries**.

Distinct from criterion 1, which is about whether scrolling is *required at all*.

### 8. Progress indicators and reading-time estimates

No peer-reviewed comprehension evidence in either direction. The large rigorous literature has
**dropout** as its dependent variable. Villar, Callegaro & Yang (2013),
`10.1177/0894439313497468` — 32 randomised experiments: the ordinary constant-speed bar does
not even reduce drop-off. Conrad, Couper, Tourangeau & Peytchev (2010),
`10.1016/j.intcom.2010.03.001` — *n* ≈ 3,000 ×2: ⚠️ **both depth-of-processing proxies were
null, twice**, and only 37.4% of the on-demand group ever clicked "Show Progress".

Omit them under criterion 6, not as an evidenced claim of their own.

⚠️ **One citation to avoid:** Mangen, Walgermo & Brønnick (2013) is routinely cited for the
"sense of the whole text" argument but **manipulated, measured and displayed no progress cue of
any kind**.

### 9. Disfluency / hard-to-read fonts

Does not replicate. Xie, Zhou & Liu (2018), `10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x` — 39 experiments,
3,135 participants: recall *d* = −0.01, transfer *d* = 0.03. Meyer et al. (2015),
`10.1037/xge0000049` — *N* = 7,367, CI [−0.06, +0.05]. Taylor et al. (2020),
`10.1080/09658211.2020.1758726` — *N* = 882, **statistical equivalence established**. Bjork &
Yue (2016): *"the remarkable and intriguing benefits … do not replicate, at least not in any
general way."* 🚩 Sans Forgetica's original claims were never peer-reviewed.

## 3. Soften these two

### Expertise reversal

**Tetzlaff, L., Simonsmeier, B., Peters, T., & Brod, G. (2025).** *Learning and Instruction,
98*, 102142. `10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142` — 60 studies, 176 effects, *N* = 5,924: low
prior knowledge *d* = 0.505; **high prior knowledge *d* = −0.428** [−0.647, −0.209];
interaction *d* = 0.971.

Three qualifiers. It is **asymmetric** — the authors advise *"rather provide assistance than
withhold it when in doubt."* True crossover concentrates in between-cohort designs (≈ −0.21 in
the modal median-split design). And **Noetel et al. (2022)**, `10.3102/00346543211052329` — 29
reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants — found prior knowledge **did not** moderate:
*"the expertise-reversal hypothesis … was not supported by our analyses."*

⚠️ **Never cite Kalyuga et al. (2003) for an effect size** — it is a theoretical synthesis.

🎯 And for a *skilled, high-knowledge* reader the direction may flip: O'Reilly & McNamara
(2007), `10.1080/01638530709336895`, *n* = 143 — the benefit of low-cohesion text was confined
to *less skilled* high-knowledge readers; **skilled high-knowledge readers benefited from HIGH
cohesion**. So keep explicit connectives rather than compressing them.

### Self-explanation

**Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018).** *EPR, 30*(3),
703–725. `10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x` — *g* = **.552** [.454, .650], *k* = 69, *N* = 5,917.

But: **41 of 69 effects use do-nothing controls** (*g* = .673, confirmed inflated); against a
*provided* explanation it is *g* = **.354**, *k* = 6. By outcome, **comprehension *g* = .282,
*k* = 3, CI [−.014, .579] — does not clear zero.** 🎯 And **every included study elicited a
response; none tested a prompt embedded without collecting an answer** — which is exactly the
optional, skippable form. ⚠️ Publication bias assessed by fail-safe *N* only.

## 4. Framing corrections

**Germane load was redefined, not dropped.** Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019),
`10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5`, verbatim: *"rather than contributing to the total load, germane
cognitive load redistributes working memory resources from extraneous activities to activities
directly relevant to learning … rather than imposing a load in its own right."* So **total load
= intrinsic + extraneous**. Element interactivity was also extended from intrinsic to *also*
extraneous load (Sweller 2010, `10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5`).

**There is no single "Mayer's effect size."** His figures are medians over his own lab and move
by up to ~70% between his own publications. Cromley & Chen (2025) re-analysed his corpus: eight
principles non-significant, overall *g* = 0.37 and declining per year. Personalization, often
quoted at *d* = 1.00, is *d* = 0.30 (retention) / 0.54 (transfer) in Ginns, Martin & Marsh
(2013), and Almeida, Münzer & Kühl (2024), `10.1111/jcal.13026`, found non-personalized
materials **better** on transfer.

**Common authorship errors** — getting these wrong undermines the rigour the artifact claims:
Schroeder & **Cenkci** (not Cepeda) · Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, **Salimi, Winne** (not Adesope) ·
Schroeder, Nesbit, **Anguiano**, Adesope · McNamara et al. (1996) is in ***Cognition and
Instruction*, 14**(1), 1–43 (not *Discourse Processes*), and it **never uses "reverse
cohesion"** — that term is O'Reilly & McNamara (2007).

## 5. Do not cite

🔴 **Unobtainable; nothing should rest on them:** Dyson & Haselgrove (2001) — the only study
supporting a line-length comprehension optimum; Ginns (2006); Kalyuga et al. (2003);
Bringhurst's primary text.

🚩 **Not peer-reviewed:** *Usability News* (Wichita State SURL in-house newsletter, the source
of much line-length lore) · Youngman & Scharff (1998) · Bringhurst and Spencer (craft books,
the actual origin of "66" and "70") · WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.8's uncited "80 characters" · Colin
Wheildon's *Type and Layout* · Nielsen Norman Group on progressive disclosure (the sole basis
for the pattern) · "8 min read" labels and Kindle "time left" (product features, never
evaluated for comprehension) · Sans Forgetica's original claims.

⚠️ **Theory, not evidence:** Larkin & Simon (1987), `10.1111/j.1551-6708.1987.tb00863.x` — no
participants, no effect sizes; the title's "(sometimes)" is load-bearing. Nesbit & Adesope
(2006) on concept maps **tests no tables** — do not cite it to justify a table; use Brick et
al. (2020). Its own headline *g* = .604 is inflated; the representational-choice branch is
*g* = .388, and ⚠️ **hyperlinked maps *g* = .017, *k* = 6 — no benefit.**

## 6. The honest bound

🎯 Noetel et al. (2022) found good design mattered **more for complex material and
system-paced settings than for self-paced ones.** A self-paced phone read is where these gains
are smallest.

Say so. The artifact is still worth building — but its durable value is usually the evidence
cards and the mis-citation field, which are *content* work, not the reading ergonomics.
