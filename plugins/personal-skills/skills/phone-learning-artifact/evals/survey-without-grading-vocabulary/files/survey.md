# A survey of community seed libraries in Northern Europe

*SYNTHETIC TEST MATERIAL. Organisations, people, places and figures are invented for evaluating
a skill. Nothing here should be cited.*

*Prepared for the Nordic Horticultural Trust, 2026. Descriptive survey; no hypothesis is tested
and no recommendation is made.*

---

## Scope

This document describes how community seed libraries in Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and
Iceland are organised. It covers governance, cataloguing, storage, lending, and funding. It is
descriptive: it does not evaluate which arrangements work better, and it does not advocate for
any of them. Sixty-one libraries responded to the questionnaire; eleven declined; an unknown
number were never reached.

---

## Governance

Most responding libraries sit inside an existing institution. Thirty-one are hosted by a public
library, fourteen by a horticultural society, nine by a school or university, and seven are
independent associations.

Hosting arrangements vary in formality. Some have a written agreement covering space, staff
time and liability; others operate on an understanding with a sympathetic librarian. The
independent associations are the only ones that hold their own insurance.

Decision-making is typically by a small committee of three to six people. Four libraries
reported having no formal decision process at all.

## Cataloguing

Three cataloguing practices were reported.

**Integration into the host catalogue.** Seeds are entered as lending items in the host
library's existing system. Twenty-two libraries do this. It makes seeds discoverable alongside
books, and constrains the fields available to describe a variety.

**A separate spreadsheet.** Twenty-six libraries keep a spreadsheet, usually shared. Fields
vary widely; the most common are variety name, year collected, donor, and quantity.

**Paper only.** Thirteen libraries keep index cards or a ledger. Several respondents noted this
suits a low-volume collection and requires no technical maintenance.

Variety naming is inconsistent across all three practices. The same variety may appear under a
common name, a vernacular name, and a supplier's trade name in neighbouring libraries.

## Storage

Reported storage ranges from a domestic refrigerator to a dedicated cool room. Thirty-four
libraries store seed at ambient room temperature in paper envelopes. Nineteen refrigerate.
Eight have humidity control.

Several respondents described seasonal pressure on space: donations peak after the autumn
harvest, which is also when storage is fullest.

## Lending and return

Most libraries ask borrowers to return seed from the plants they grow, but almost none enforce
it. Reported return rates, where tracked at all, ranged from under 5% to about 40%. Twenty-nine
libraries do not track returns.

Three lending models appear. Open access, where anyone may take seed with no record, is used by
eighteen libraries. Membership lending, requiring a free or paid membership, is used by
twenty-seven. Event-based distribution, where seed is given out only at swap days, is used by
sixteen.

## Funding

Reported annual budgets ranged from nothing to about €4,000. Twenty-three libraries reported no
budget at all and run on donated seed and volunteer time. The remainder are funded by host
institutions, municipal grants, membership fees, or occasional project grants.

Respondents frequently mentioned that project grants fund setup but not continuation, and that
replacing a failed refrigerator is harder to fund than starting a new library.

## Volunteers

Libraries reported between one and twenty-two active volunteers, with a median of four. The
most commonly reported difficulty was continuity: in nineteen libraries a single person held
most of the operational knowledge.

## Legal context

Respondents described varying understanding of seed marketing rules. Because the libraries
generally lend or give rather than sell, most considered themselves outside commercial seed
regulation, but several noted they had never confirmed this. Two had sought formal advice. This
survey did not assess the legal position and takes no view on it.

## Regional notes

Icelandic libraries reported the shortest growing season and the heaviest reliance on imported
seed. Finnish libraries most often reported integration with public libraries. Danish libraries
reported the highest proportion of independent associations. No comparison between countries
was tested, and these are descriptive counts from an unrepresentative sample.

---

## What this survey does not contain

No outcome was measured. The survey did not ask whether a library's arrangements worked well,
did not compare arrangements, and did not follow up. Response was voluntary and the sample is
not representative: libraries that had recently closed were, by construction, unlikely to
respond.
