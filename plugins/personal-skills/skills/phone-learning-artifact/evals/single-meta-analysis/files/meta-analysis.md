# Meta-analysis: workplace daylight exposure and self-reported alertness

*SYNTHETIC TEST MATERIAL. Authors, venues, identifiers and findings are invented for evaluating
a skill. Nothing here should be cited.*

**Hollenbeck, Ruiz & Adeyemi (2026).** *Journal of Environmental Psychology* (synthetic),
`10.0000/synthetic.2026.0041`

---

## Abstract

Random-effects meta-analysis of 48 studies (k = 61 effects, N = 14,302) on the association
between measured workplace daylight exposure and self-reported alertness. Pooled
*g* = **0.31** [0.21, 0.41], *I²* = 68.4%. The effect is moderated by measurement method and by
study design, and is not moderated by latitude or by season of data collection.

---

## Method

Searched four databases to January 2026. Included: adult workers, a measured (not
self-reported) daylight exposure variable, and a validated alertness instrument. Excluded:
shift-work populations, clinical samples, and studies where lighting was manipulated as part of
a broader intervention.

Effect sizes converted to Hedges' *g*. Random-effects model, restricted maximum likelihood.
Risk of bias assessed with a 7-item instrument; two raters, κ = 0.78.

---

## Results

### Pooled estimate

*g* = 0.31 [0.21, 0.41], *p* < .001, *I²* = 68.4%, τ² = 0.09, 95% prediction interval
[−0.09, 0.71]. **Note the prediction interval crosses zero**: a new study in a new setting may
well find no effect.

### Moderators

| Moderator | Levels | *g* | Significant? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Measurement method | wearable dosimetry | 0.44 [0.31, 0.57] | **yes**, *p* = .003 |
| | workstation lux proxy | 0.19 [0.06, 0.32] | |
| Study design | within-subject | 0.42 [0.28, 0.56] | **yes**, *p* = .01 |
| | between-subject | 0.21 [0.09, 0.33] | |
| Alertness instrument | KSS | 0.33 | no, *p* = .44 |
| | SOFI | 0.28 | |
| Latitude | above/below 45° | 0.30 / 0.32 | no, *p* = .81 |
| Season | winter / other | 0.28 / 0.33 | no, *p* = .62 |
| Risk of bias | low / moderate / high | 0.22 / 0.33 / 0.47 | **yes**, *p* = .02 |

The risk-of-bias gradient is the uncomfortable one: **higher-bias studies report larger
effects**. Restricting to low-bias studies gives *g* = 0.22 [0.10, 0.34], still significant but
materially smaller than the headline.

### Small-study effects

Egger's test *p* = .04. Trim-and-fill imputes 7 studies and adjusts the pooled estimate to
*g* = 0.24 [0.13, 0.35].

## Included studies (selected)

- **Agboola & Lindström (2019).** Within-subject, wearable dosimetry, N = 88. *g* = 0.51. Low
  risk of bias. The single most-cited study in this literature. *Frequently cited for a causal
  claim; it is observational, and the authors say so.*
- **Petrova et al. (2021).** Between-subject, lux proxy, N = 1,204. *g* = 0.09, n.s. The largest
  study and a near-null. *Routinely omitted from narrative reviews of this literature.*
- **Castellanos (2020).** Within-subject, dosimetry, N = 42. *g* = 0.78, the largest effect in
  the set. High risk of bias (no blinding, alertness measured by the investigator). *Do not cite
  the 0.78 without the bias rating.*
- **Nkemelu & Harrison (2023).** Between-subject, dosimetry, N = 610. *g* = 0.28. Low risk.
- **Varga & Oduya (2022).** Within-subject, lux proxy, N = 155. *g* = 0.19. Moderate risk.
  *Reports a seasonal interaction that did not replicate in this meta-analysis's moderator test.*
- **Three studies (Weller 2018; Jaffar 2019; Ruiz 2021)** share an overlapping cohort. Only
  Weller is included; including all three would double-count 340 participants. *Several
  secondary sources treat them as independent.*

---

## Limitations stated by the authors

1. All outcomes are **self-reported** alertness. No objective performance measure was eligible,
   so the pooled estimate speaks to perceived alertness only.
2. **Causal direction is not established.** Every included study is observational; alert
   workers may seek out daylit space.
3. The prediction interval crosses zero.
4. The risk-of-bias gradient and the small-study effect point the same way, and the authors
   write that the true effect "is plausibly nearer 0.20 than 0.31."
5. Shift workers were excluded, which is where the effect might matter most.

---

## What the authors conclude

A small positive association, most defensible when exposure is measured with dosimetry and the
design is within-subject. They explicitly decline to recommend lighting interventions on this
evidence, noting that the intervention literature was excluded by design and that a
within-subject association is not an intervention effect.
